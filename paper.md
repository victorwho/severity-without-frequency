# Severity Without Frequency: A Multi-Country Validation of a Cycling Routing Risk Score

## Abstract

**Research Problem and Approach:** Cycling-safety routing products increasingly steer riders using a composite, per-segment risk score, but such scores are rarely validated against exposure-adjusted injury outcomes, and where they are, crash frequency and severity are almost always validated as one outcome. This paper tests whether a deployed cycling routing risk score predicts frequency and severity differently, once cyclist exposure is accounted for.

**Methodology and Findings:** Negative-binomial regression with region-clustered standard errors and city fixed effects was applied to a five-country, 13-region counter-exposure panel of 2,034 sites recording approximately 3.98 billion cyclist passages, cross-checked against Poisson-HC1 estimation and leave-one-region-out analysis, and replicated under a later, independently re-scored model generation with road-class control and a mid-block-versus-junction split. The score's relationship to raw crash frequency was weak and inconsistent across estimators and cut-points. Its relationship to crash severity (killed-or-seriously-injured outcomes) was significant at every cut-point, strengthened under the later generation, and, for the first time across the generations examined, survived full road-class control, concentrated at mid-block locations rather than junctions.

**Key Contributions:** This paper makes three contributions: (1) within the literature searched, the first validation of a deployed composite cycling risk score testing frequency and severity as separable outcomes at multi-country, multi-generation scale; (2) evidence that road-class control, not exposure adjustment alone, is needed to distinguish a genuine severity signal from a road-type proxy; and (3) concrete guidance for the routing product this score serves, including which public claim the evidence supports and how many display bands the data can separate.

**Implications:** Validating a risk score as one undifferentiated "crash risk" outcome can obscure a real, mechanistically grounded severity signal beneath an apparently weak pooled result; a kinetic-energy mechanism together with severity-dependent crash underreporting plausibly explains the asymmetry found. In practice, a road scored worse by this system should be described as more likely to produce a severe outcome if a crash occurs, not as more likely to see a crash occur at all, and the display scale should carry no more resolution than the data supports.

**Keywords:** cycling safety, exposure-adjusted crash risk, road-class control, severity versus frequency, routing risk score

# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Hook and context

Cycling-safety routing products increasingly steer riders using a composite, per-segment risk score computed before any journey is taken, rather than relying solely on a cyclist's own judgement or on crash statistics compiled after the fact. Such products are typically built on top of open, continent-scale road network data and real-time routing engines [1], which makes it straightforward to attach a per-segment score to a route calculation but says nothing about whether that score is accurate. The underlying premise is straightforward: if a road's danger can be estimated in advance from measurable characteristics — how fast motor traffic moves, how much of it there is, whether protective cycling infrastructure is present — a route can be planned to avoid the worst of it. That premise depends entirely on whether the estimate is accurate. A composite score built from plausible, expert-selected ingredients is not the same thing as a score whose relationship to real injury outcomes has actually been tested, and the two are easy to conflate in a product context where the score already exists and is already influencing routes.

## 1.2 Problem statement

Testing that relationship is harder than counting crashes on scored roads and seeing whether the count lines up with the score, and the reason is a well-documented denominator problem: crash counts mostly reflect where people choose to cycle, not where cycling is dangerous. A quiet residential street carrying a thousand riders a day will record more crashes than a fast arterial almost nobody dares to use, simply because there are more riders present to have them, and a road-level score validated against raw counts alone will systematically mistake exposure for danger. This is not a hypothetical concern. Safety performance functions built without pedestrian and bicycle exposure terms have been shown to identify a different, and in some cases less equitable, set of high-risk locations than the same functions built with exposure included [2]. Exposure-controlled analysis of Copenhagen-area bicycle crashes has shown that accounting for detailed flow substantially changes which conditions appear associated with elevated risk [3], and exposure-adjusted crash-rate frameworks built around finer-grained conditions such as lighting and time-of-day have been shown to reveal risk variation an aggregate, unadjusted rate would miss entirely [4]. Bicycle-specific exposure-based crash-rate work in the Central Lane, Oregon metropolitan region, a separate mixed-methods investigation of bicycle exposure in crash rates, and a study of the roles infrastructure and land use play in both bicycle crash exposure and frequency have each examined some facet of this same denominator question [5][6][7]. None of this is specific to any one of those studies. The point that follows from the denominator problem generally is that a score validated only against how often a road is crashed on, without also asking how much it is cycled on, risks describing exposure rather than danger.

Even where a study does account for exposure, a second and less-examined problem remains: "crash risk" is almost always validated as a single, undifferentiated outcome. A score is asked whether it predicts crashes, full stop, rather than being asked two separable questions — does it predict whether a crash happens at all, and does it predict how badly that crash ends. These are not the same question. There are good mechanistic reasons to expect a road-level score to answer them differently. A large literature in road-safety engineering treats vehicle speed and impact kinetic energy as the dominant determinant of injury severity once a collision occurs. Safe System research identifies critical impact-speed thresholds beyond which the probability of a fatal or serious injury rises sharply [8], and historical natural-experiment evidence shows that a speed-limit reduction can cut severe and fatal outcomes by a much larger margin than it cuts total crash counts [9]. Whether a collision occurs in the first place is a different question, governed by junction geometry, sightlines, and split-second driver and rider behaviour — a largely separate set of factors that a road-level score built substantially from speed and traffic inputs has little obvious mechanism to capture.

A score built substantially from exactly those speed and traffic ingredients should, on this reasoning, be a better predictor of severity than of frequency, yet the published cycling-safety literature searched for this paper does not test the two separately for the same score; frequency and severity are folded into one outcome, or severity alone is modelled without a frequency comparison [10][11][12]. A further complication, documented directly for cyclists, is that official crash records systematically undercount minor injuries far more than serious ones. One study of linked police and hospital records found cyclist injuries reported to police at roughly 7% for slight injuries versus roughly 14% for serious ones in the same population [13]. That gap alone would tend to attenuate any true frequency effect in official-record data while leaving a severity effect comparatively intact, independently of anything mechanistic about the score itself. Nor does any source found validate a deployed, multi-factor composite score against exposure-adjusted outcomes at more than single-city scale; the closest comparators are single-city case-crossover studies of infrastructure and injury risk [14][15] or a single-city composite road-safety index validated against crash prediction [16].

## 1.3 Research question

This paper asks, for one deployed, safety-weighted cycling routing risk score, whether it predicts (a) raw crash frequency and (b) crash severity — specifically, killed-or-seriously-injured (KSI) outcomes — once exposure is properly accounted for, and whether the answers to (a) and (b) differ. Three further questions follow from that primary one. Does any severity signal found survive statistical control for road classification, given that a score partly built from road-type-correlated inputs could otherwise be predicting nothing more than "this is a major road"? Is any surviving signal concentrated at particular locations — mid-block segments versus junctions — given that a segment-level score has no obvious mechanism for capturing junction-specific danger? And does the pattern found in one generation of the score persist, or change, in a later, independently re-scored generation of the same underlying methodology?

## 1.4 Contribution

This paper answers those questions using a five-country, 13-region counter-exposure panel of 2,034 sites recording approximately 3.98 billion cyclist passages, 10,399 crashes, and 1,169 KSI outcomes, analyzed with negative-binomial regression, region-clustered robust standard errors, and city fixed effects, cross-checked against a Poisson-HC1 specification and a leave-one-region-out influence analysis. The same analysis is repeated under a later model generation at both fixed and percentile-matched score thresholds, with an added road-class control, a mid-block-versus-junction split, a per-named-factor audit, and E-value bounds on the strength of confounding that would be needed to explain the findings away.

The headline pattern previewed here, and reported in full in Section 3, is a dissociation between the two outcomes. The score's relationship to raw crash frequency is weak, inconsistent across estimators, and does not survive road-class control in either generation tested. Its relationship to crash severity is the opposite: significant at every cut-point tested, strengthening rather than weakening under the later model generation, and — for the first time across the generations examined here — surviving full road-class control, with the surviving signal concentrated at mid-block locations rather than at junctions. This paper also reports, as secondary evidence subjected to its own country-level robustness check, cross-city evidence bearing on whether this pattern generalizes beyond the panel's own roads. Throughout, only the statistical relationship between the score and outcomes is reported; the underlying scoring weights are proprietary to the routing product and are never disclosed, consistent with how this project already discloses factor information to the public.

## 1.5 Organization

Section 2 describes the counter-exposure panel, the two model generations compared, and the statistical methods used, including the road-class control, the mid-block/junction split, the per-factor audit, and the sensitivity analysis. Section 3 reports the frequency and severity contrasts, the road-class-controlled and location-split results, the per-factor audit, and the secondary city-level evidence. Section 4 interprets these findings against candidate mechanisms and the surrounding literature, states this paper's limitations, and considers what they imply for how a deployed cycling risk score should be validated and used. Section 5 concludes.

# 2. Methods

## 2.1 Data

The primary evidence in this paper came from a counter-exposure panel assembled to test a deployed, safety-weighted cycling routing risk score against real injury outcomes rather than against raw crash counts, which are confounded by where people choose to cycle rather than by how dangerous a road actually is. The panel combined two kinds of public record across five countries: official police and government crash records, and passively-collected cyclist counts from automatic counting installations supplemented by hand counts performed by city planners. In total the panel covered 2,034 counting sites across 13 regions in Germany, Switzerland, Belgium, France, and Spain, recording approximately 3.98 billion cyclist passages, 10,399 crashes, and 1,169 killed-or-seriously-injured (KSI) outcomes (*source: validation/out/panel_final.csv, summarized via validation/paper_analysis.py, reproduced in this pipeline*). The 13 regions were Baden-Württemberg, Basel, Belgium, a second independent Belgian counter network ("Belgium-new"), Berlin, a second independent Berlin network ("Berlin-BZM"), Bordeaux, Madrid, Nantes, Paris, a French Telraam network, Toulouse, and Zurich.

A design feature of this panel is worth stating plainly rather than leaving implicit, because it bears on how its statistical power should be read. Permanent automatic counters are disproportionately installed on pleasant, protected routes cities want to promote, not on roads a risk-weighted score would flag as dangerous; an audit of counters across four cities in this project's own prior work found that none of 111 permanent installations sat on a road this project's score rates as genuinely dangerous. Hand counts, placed by planners on busy main roads for traffic-management purposes, partially corrected this gap, but the panel still likely under-sampled the most dangerous end of the network. This limits how far the findings extrapolate to the very worst roads (Section 4.7), rather than being a defect specific to this analysis.

## 2.2 Score generations compared

Two generations of the same underlying scoring methodology were compared in this paper. The first was the score generation originally published and validated against this panel (referred to below as the original generation); the second was a later generation, internally designated b46v1, that incorporated several refinements to the scoring methodology accumulated since the original validation. Because the underlying weights and their point-value magnitudes are proprietary to the routing product this score serves, this paper reports only the statistical relationship between each generation's score and real-world outcomes — relative risks, confidence intervals, p-values, and the sign (protective or harmful) of individual named scoring factors — and never the point-value magnitudes assigned to any factor. This is a deliberate reporting choice, not an omission the reader should read as concealment: it mirrors how this project already discloses factor information to the public, through a service that returns only the sign of each named factor's contribution.

Because the two generations' numeric scales are not directly comparable — the later generation's refinements shift the distribution of scores relative to the original — every comparison across generations in Section 3 was made either at literal matching cut-points (the same numeric threshold applied to both generations) or at percentile-matched cut-points (a threshold chosen in the later generation to select the same share of sites as the corresponding threshold did in the original generation). Both are reported side by side rather than only one, so that a reader can judge whether an apparent change in effect size reflects a real change in the score's predictive content or only a shift in its numeric scale.

## 2.3 Statistical model

The core statistical model was a negative binomial regression (NB2, with the dispersion parameter $\alpha$ estimated by maximum likelihood rather than fixed) of crash counts on the risk score, with log-passages entered as an exposure offset, city fixed effects, and a free exposure elasticity. Two outcomes were modeled separately in every specification: all recorded crashes, and the KSI subset. The choice of NB2 follows standard practice for overdispersed crash-count data [17], and was confirmed rather than assumed here: a Poisson diagnostic run on the same panel found substantial overdispersion for the all-crash outcome (deviance/df $\approx 5.1$, Pearson $\chi^2$/df $\approx 8.7$) but a KSI outcome close to Poisson-consistent (deviance/df $\approx 0.95$, Pearson $\chi^2$/df $\approx 1.4$) (*source: validation/paper_analysis.py output, this pipeline*), a first indication — before any relative-risk estimate was examined — that the two outcomes behaved differently.

Standard errors were clustered by region throughout, alongside model-based standard errors reported for comparison, and a Poisson model with HC1 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors was reported as an estimator-robustness cross-check on every contrast. One caveat applies to every cluster-robust confidence interval reported in Section 3: the panel's 13 regions vary enormously in size, from a few dozen sites to several hundred, and simulation evidence from the econometrics literature shows that conventional cluster-robust inference performs markedly worse under exactly this kind of imbalance than under a balanced cluster structure of the same count, with the commonly-invoked "rule of 42" heuristic (that 42 or more clusters is enough for reliable inference) failing to hold for unbalanced cluster sizes [18]. A wild-cluster-bootstrap procedure is the standard remedy [19][20] and was not run here; the cluster-robust intervals in Section 3 should accordingly be read as approximate, a point returned to in Section 4.7.

Negative-binomial regression applied to categorical crash-contributing factors is itself well-precedented: a comparable specification on Jordanian crash-severity data found severity decreasing significantly with driver age and increasing significantly with driver fault [21], illustrating the factor-level inference the same tool supports.

## 2.4 Threshold and matching strategy

In the original generation, one contrast was pre-specified as primary (score $\geq 65$ versus $<65$) and two were exploratory (score $\geq 80$ versus $<80$; and a restricted comparison of score $>100$ against score $<30$, dropping the middle of the distribution to sharpen the contrast between low- and high-scored sites). In the later (b46v1) generation, the same four cut-points (30, 65, 80, 100) were re-applied twice: as fixed absolute thresholds on the new score scale, and as percentile-matched thresholds chosen so each cut selected the same proportion of sites the corresponding original-generation cut did. Every contrast was run for both outcomes, under all three estimators from Section 2.3.

## 2.5 Road-class control

Because the risk score is itself partly a function of road classification, and because major roads differ systematically from minor ones in both crash exposure and outcome severity, every contrast above was re-estimated with an added set of ordered road-class dummies (major, mid, minor, derived from the OpenStreetMap `highway` carriageway tag of each site) to test whether the score carried information beyond road class alone. Conditioning on a variable the score is partly built from is a conservative test by construction — it can only remove signal, never add spurious signal — and is reported as such rather than as a neutral robustness check. A subset of the panel that carries a mid-block/junction distinction was further split to test whether any surviving KSI signal was concentrated at one or the other.

## 2.6 Per-factor audit

To examine which named scoring factors carry the aggregate score's KSI signal, a univariate presence/absence audit was run separately within each of three countries with sufficient within-country crash counts to support it (Germany, France, Belgium): for each named factor, the KSI rate ratio between sites where that factor is present and sites where it is absent, with the false-discovery rate controlled across the factors tested within each country. As with the aggregate score, only each factor's statistical relationship to KSI — relative risk, confidence interval, p-value, and false-discovery-rate flag — is reported here; the point-value weight the scoring formula assigns to each factor is proprietary and is not reported anywhere in this paper.

## 2.7 Sensitivity analysis

For the primary and exploratory contrasts in the original score generation, E-value sensitivity bounds [22] were computed at both the point estimate and the confidence-interval bound, quantifying the minimum strength an unmeasured confounder would need — on the risk-ratio scale, with both the confounder-exposure and confounder-outcome associations at least that strong — to fully explain away the observed association. No usable identifier was found in this project's literature search for VanderWeele and Ding's commonly-cited 2017 paper specifically introducing the E-value; the method here instead followed the general bias-formula framework it built on [22] and a later refinement showing that E-value bounds are not always sharp (tight), which should be read as a bound on how much confounding would be required rather than a precise threshold [23].

## 2.8 City-level index (secondary evidence)

As a secondary line of evidence on whether the score's predictive content generalizes across, rather than only within, cities, this paper also reports results from a separately-published multi-city cycling risk index that aggregates road-level scores to one number per city across a 117-city set, built from the same b46v1-generation risk export used throughout this paper (*source: ccri_pilot/results117s/index117.json*, confirmed byte-identical to the archived `results117s_46ct` snapshot of the currently-published baseline). Both the city rankings and the criterion validation — whether a city's index value actually predicts its real KSI rate per cyclist passage — are reported from this same current generation, using the point-in-polygon site-to-city attribution built into the index's own criterion script (`ccri_pilot/eu117_criterion.py`), rerun for this paper. As with the road-level score in Sections 2.5 and 3.7, the criterion association is additionally tested with an analogous control — country fixed effects — to test whether an apparent city-to-city gradient survives once between-country differences are accounted for. Unlike the road-class control applied to the road-level score, that control is not favorable to the index; the result, reported in full in Section 3.11, is disclosed rather than omitted.

## 3.2 Overdispersion diagnostic

Before any relative-risk contrast was examined, a Poisson diagnostic run on the panel showed the two outcomes behaved differently in a way that motivates the negative-binomial specification used throughout. The all-crash outcome was substantially overdispersed (Poisson deviance/df $\approx 5.1$; Pearson $\chi^2$/df $\approx 8.7$), while the KSI outcome was close to Poisson-consistent (deviance/df $\approx 0.95$; Pearson $\chi^2$/df $\approx 1.4$) (*source: validation/paper_analysis.py output*). Overdispersion of this magnitude in the all-crash outcome is itself informative: it indicates unmodeled heterogeneity in what drives ordinary crashes that the KSI outcome does not share to the same degree.

The pattern in Table 2 is the paper's central empirical observation, and it appeared before any further adjustment was applied. The all-crash outcome was null or only marginally significant at every cut-point, and its significance was inconsistent across estimators: the pre-specified primary contrast ($\geq$65) did not reach significance under any of the three estimators. The KSI outcome, by contrast, was significant under every estimator at every cut-point, and rose monotonically as the threshold tightened, from RR 1.39 at $\geq$65 to RR 2.19 at the restricted $>$100-vs.-$<$30 contrast. The E-value bounds rose in step: explaining away the KSI association at the tightest cut-point would require an unmeasured confounder associated with both the score and KSI outcome by a risk ratio of at least 3.80 at the point estimate, and 2.53 even at the lower confidence bound — a substantially stronger confound than would be needed to explain away any of the all-crash associations.

## 3.4 Leave-one-region-out

Dropping each of the 13 regions in turn from the KSI $\geq$65 contrast left the relative risk stable in both sign and rough magnitude: RR ranged from 1.34 (dropping Berlin or the independent "Belgium-new" network) to 1.44 (dropping Baden-Württemberg, Bordeaux, or Zurich), with every one of the 13 drops remaining significant at p$\leq$.004 (*source: validation/paper_analysis.py output*). The same held for the restricted $>$100-vs.-$<$30 KSI contrast: RR ranged from 1.74 (dropping Berlin-BZM) to 2.76 (dropping Baden-Württemberg), with every drop significant at p$\leq$.005. No single region drove the KSI association.

The road-class control is the paper's most direct test of whether the score's severity signal was more than a proxy for "this is a major road." Under the original generation, every KSI contrast's significance was fully attenuated once class was controlled for: the $\geq$65-equivalent effect fell from a significant RR of 1.36 to a non-significant RR of 1.17, and the other two cuts followed the same pattern. Under the b46v1 generation, by contrast, the $\geq$65-equivalent and $\geq$80-equivalent KSI effects survived class control at conventional significance (RR 1.42, p<.001, and RR 1.48, p=.028, respectively), and the $\geq$100-equivalent effect, while no longer reaching p<.05 (p=.088), retained a point estimate above 1.5. This was, within the two generations examined here, the first to survive full road-class control on the complete panel at more than one cut-point.

## 3.8 Mid-block vs. junction split, class-controlled

On the subset of the panel carrying a mid-block (link) versus junction distinction (n=1,320; link KSI=400, junction KSI=356), the class-controlled b46v1 KSI signal was concentrated at mid-block locations. The per-standard-deviation class-controlled effect was RR 1.30 [1.09, 1.56], p=.004 at mid-block links, versus RR 1.06 [0.91, 1.24], p=.458 at junctions (*source: rescore_class_control_b46.txt*). At the $\geq$100-equivalent cut, the mid-block class-controlled effect strengthened further to RR 1.92 [1.05, 3.53], p=.035, while the junction effect remained non-significant at every cut tested. The same pattern held under the original generation's score (mid-block per-SD RR 1.38 [1.14, 1.68], p=.001; junction per-SD RR 1.05 [0.91, 1.21], p=.502), so the mid-block concentration was not specific to the later generation. All-crash effects were non-significant at both location types under class control, in both generations, at every cut except the b46v1 $\geq$100-equivalent junction contrast, which was marginal (RR 1.31, p=.063).

## 3.10 Band-count support

The b46v1 generation's currently-published eight-band display scale showed three adjacent-band inversions (an adjacent band with a lower adjusted KSI rate ratio than the band below it) and zero pairs of adjacent bands whose 95% confidence intervals were fully separated, out of a possible seven adjacent pairs (*source: rescore_reanchor_b46.txt*). A five-band scale with cuts at 42, 80, 105, and 130 showed zero inversions across the panel, with an adjusted KSI rate ratio ladder of 1.00 (reference, sites scoring below 42) $\to$ 1.04 [0.86, 1.25] $\to$ 1.27 [0.97, 1.67] $\to$ 1.57 [1.20, 2.07] $\to$ 1.94 [1.50, 2.52]. Under that five-band structure, 9.35% of network kilometers fell in the lowest band, 77.13% in the second, 10.13% in the third, 2.38% in the fourth, and 1.00% in the highest band. The published eight-band scale therefore claims more resolution among the panel's middle risk range than the outcome data can currently separate.

## 3.11 City-level cross-country evidence (secondary, country-control-tested)

As secondary evidence on cross-city generalization, the currently-published 117-city cycling risk index, built from the same b46v1 risk export used throughout this paper, ranked Stockholm highest (index 98.3 of 100) and Buzău lowest (index 0.6), with the 117 cities divided into three tiers of 39 cities each (*source: ccri_pilot/results117s/index117.json*).

Point-in-polygon attribution of the counter-exposure panel's geocoded sites (Section 2.1) to these city boundaries recovers outcome data for 12 of the 117 indexed cities, spanning five countries (Germany, Switzerland, Belgium, France, Spain) (*source: ccri_pilot/eu117_criterion.py output against results117s, reproduced in this pipeline*). Across those 12 cities, the index correlates with each city's real KSI rate per cyclist passage in the hypothesized direction: Spearman $\rho = -0.720$ (higher index, denoting a safer city, associated with a lower KSI rate), and a negative-binomial regression of city-level KSI counts on the index, with log-passages entered as an offset, gives a rate ratio of 0.74 [0.67, 0.82] per +10 index points ($p<.001$). The all-crash outcome, by contrast, shows no association with the index (RR 0.98 [0.90, 1.07] per +10 points, $p=.613$) — the same frequency/severity dissociation found at the road level in Sections 3.3-3.9 reappears, marginally, at the city level.

That marginal association does not survive the same kind of control this paper applies to the road-level score in Section 3.7. Adding country fixed effects to the same regression collapses the KSI rate ratio to 1.00 [0.86, 1.16] ($p=.983$): once which country a city sits in is accounted for, the index carries no further information about that city's KSI rate. Within-country ordering points to the same conclusion by a different route. Of the four countries with more than one indexed city in the outcome data, three show an index ranking inconsistent with the real KSI ordering: Belgium (Antwerp 93/2.12, Brussels 92/1.16, Ghent 84/1.57 KSI per 10M passages — the highest-indexed city is not the safest), Germany (Berlin 71/4.67 vs. Stuttgart 53/2.27 — the lower-indexed city is safer), and France (Paris 94/1.93, Toulouse 82/1.79, Nantes 80/1.35, Bordeaux 60/4.01 — non-monotonic). Only Switzerland's two cities order consistently with the index (Basel 54/5.66, Zurich 52/7.40). This is the reverse of the road-level result in Section 3.7, where the score's severity signal survived an analogous confound check; here, the city-level index's marginal association with KSI appears to be substantially, perhaps entirely, a between-country pattern rather than genuine city-to-city discrimination.

# 4. Discussion

## 4.1 Interpretation

The central finding of this paper is a dissociation between two outcomes that a validation study of this kind almost never separates. A safety-weighted cycling routing risk score built substantially from motor-vehicle speed and traffic-volume inputs is a weak and statistically unstable predictor of raw crash frequency, but a robust, dose-response predictor of crash severity. This is not a single fragile estimate: it is present across three estimators (NB2 with model-based standard errors, NB2 with region-clustered standard errors, Poisson with HC1 standard errors) at every threshold tested in the original generation, it is stable under leave-one-region-out across all 13 regions, and it persists — strengthening rather than weakening at matched percentile cut-points — under an independently re-scored later model generation. It is also, for the first time across the two generations examined here, robust to full road-class control on the complete panel, and the surviving signal concentrates at mid-block locations rather than at junctions. Taken together, these are not the marks of a specification artifact; they are the marks of a real, if partial, relationship between the score and severe outcomes specifically.

The same panel, the same estimators, and the same threshold structure produce a null-to-marginal frequency result and a strong, monotonic severity result. That pairing is itself the finding worth dwelling on, because it is the opposite of what a single undifferentiated "does the score work" validation would have reported. A validation that pooled all crashes together, as the surrounding literature almost universally does, would have found a weak, inconsistently significant association and reasonably concluded the score performs only modestly. Separating the two outcomes tells a different story. The weak pooled result is not weak evidence about the score in general. It is a strong severity result diluted by a genuinely absent frequency result, and the two should not have been averaged together in the first place.

## 4.2 Mechanism

Two non-exclusive mechanisms are consistent with this pattern, and this paper's data cannot fully separate them. The first is the kinetic-energy mechanism that Safe System road-safety doctrine treats as central: vehicle speed and impact energy govern how badly a collision ends far more directly than they govern whether a collision happens at all [24]. Quantitative work in this tradition finds that impact speeds near 30 km/h correspond to roughly a 10% probability of a fatal-or-serious outcome in right-angle and head-on collisions, with the comparable threshold lower still, around 20 km/h, for pedestrians [8], and Swedish accident-site data show injury severity and fatality risk rising with measured travel speed even though the relationship is graded rather than a sharp cutoff — severe injuries remain rare but not absent well below 35 km/h [25]. A historical natural experiment offers a close precedent for the shape of this paper's own finding: after Zurich reduced its speed limit from 60 to 50 km/h, car-pedestrian crashes fell 16%, injured pedestrians fell 20%, and fatalities fell 25%, with the most severe injury category falling 86% — a much larger reduction in severe outcomes than in crash counts, attributed by the study's authors almost entirely to the speed change [9]. A score built from exactly the ingredients that mechanism implicates — speed, traffic volume, on-road stress — predicting severity better than frequency is close to what this mechanism would predict outright.

The second candidate mechanism is measurement, not physics: severity-dependent underreporting in the crash records this paper's panel is built from. Linked police-and-hospital record data for cyclists specifically show reporting rates of roughly 14% for serious injuries against roughly 7% for slight injuries in the same population [13], a pattern that builds on an established methodological lineage of linking hospital and police crash-casualty records — from early work on the feasibility of the linkage itself to later work validating its use for analysing specific injury-crash characteristics [26][27]. Even crowdsourced collision reports have been shown to diverge systematically from official insurance records by location and facility type [28], indicating that no single record source captures crashes uniformly. If minor cyclist crashes are captured in official records only about half as reliably as serious ones, a true frequency effect of the risk score would be attenuated toward null in exactly the data this paper uses, while the severity effect — measured on the much more completely captured KSI outcome — would survive largely intact. This paper's design cannot distinguish a true absence of a frequency effect from a true, smaller frequency effect attenuated by this reporting gradient, and the honest reading is that both the kinetic-energy mechanism and the underreporting mechanism plausibly contribute to the pattern observed, in proportions this paper's data do not identify.

## 4.3 Relation to the literature

This paper's central finding extends, rather than originates, the pattern documented in [9]: a speed-linked intervention or score producing a disproportionately larger effect on severe outcomes than on total crash counts. What this paper adds is a multi-country replication of that asymmetry for cyclists specifically, at panel scale, across two independent model generations, with formal road-class control — a combination the Zurich study's single-city before-after design could not offer. It also extends the same exposure-control goal pursued for Copenhagen bicycle crashes using a Palm-distribution technique applied to weather and time-of-day conditions [3] — controlling for how much cycling exposure a given condition actually receives before drawing a risk conclusion from it — to a different technique (passage-offset negative-binomial regression) applied to a composite segment-level score evaluated across five countries, rather than claiming to replicate that study's specific statistical method.

Where this paper's per-factor findings meet the literature, they meet it in genuine disagreement rather than confirmation. The per-factor audit in Section 3.9 finds cycle infrastructure protective in Germany and surviving road-class control there, consistent with London case-crossover evidence that protected cycling infrastructure reduced injury odds by 40-65% relative to no infrastructure [14]. But a Britain-wide case-crossover study using a closely comparable design over the same period found the opposite for painted cycle lanes specifically — a 54% increase in injury odds, with no protective infrastructure effect detected at all [15]. This is not an isolated disagreement. A quasi-experimental study of Berlin's pop-up bike lanes, a causal-framework evaluation of protected bike-lane treatments at intersections, a recent systematic review of bicycle infrastructure design and crash outcomes, and earlier GIS-based work on both the environmental correlates of bicycle crashes in crash-concentrated areas and on built-environment effects on cyclist injury severity have all examined some version of this same question [29][30][31][32][33]. Between them they cover a scale and a diversity of designs this paper's own reading of two studies cannot survey. This paper's data cannot adjudicate the London/Britain disagreement, and it should not be read as doing so; it can only note that its own finding sits on one side of an unresolved, same-country debate about a factor this paper reports by sign rather than by infrastructure sub-type. This paper's finding that "use of an adjacent sidepath" is harmful (RR>1) in both Germany and Belgium is consistent with the general caution the London/Britain disagreement raises: infrastructure effects are evidently sensitive to exactly which infrastructure sub-type and country is being measured, and a single named factor's sign in this paper's audit should not be over-generalized beyond the countries in which it was tested.

## 4.4 Junction risk as an unaddressed limitation

The mid-block/junction split in Section 3.8 is a direct answer to a question the score's design does not obviously address on its own. Junctions are where a large share of urban cycling collisions occur: London case-crossover evidence finds junctions carry roughly three times the injury odds of other locations [14]. Britain-wide evidence finds junction-type effects that are large but inconsistent — standard and mini roundabouts carrying substantially higher injury odds than other junction types, while traffic signals show no detectable effect at all [15]. Against that backdrop, this paper's finding that the class-controlled KSI signal is essentially confined to mid-block locations, in both model generations, is a limitation stated plainly rather than a caveat to soften: the score, and by extension the routing decisions built on it, has little demonstrated ability to distinguish a dangerous junction from a safe one, and this paper's evidence does not support claiming otherwise.

## 4.5 Practical implications

For the routing product this score serves, the practical implication is a change in which claim is made publicly, not a change in the underlying score. The evidence in Section 3 supports the claim that roads scored worse by this system carry a materially higher probability that a crash occurring there will be severe; it does not support the claim that roads scored worse are more likely to see a crash occur at all, and that distinction should be reflected in how the score is described to users rather than left implicit. This matters for more than transparency, because cyclists do act on this kind of information. A Level of Traffic Stress study found that an exclusively low-stress route was unavailable for 51% of observed trips, and that available low-stress routes ran 74% longer on average than the shortest possible path [34]. Revealed-preference evidence from Germany found that higher accident risk along a route has a measurable, if slight, negative influence on route choice [35], alongside separate choice-experiment evidence for a safety-in-numbers effect on route choice [36]. A score cyclists partly trust and route around is a score whose overclaimed half — the frequency claim this paper's evidence does not support — has a real behavioural cost, not just a rhetorical one. The band-count finding in Section 3.10 carries a second, more concrete implication: a display scale with more bands than the outcome data can separate is not a neutral design choice, it communicates a precision the evidence does not have, and a five-band structure with cuts near 42, 80, 105, and 130 is the finest grain this panel currently supports without producing an inversion. Finally, this paper's own reporting choice — publishing each named factor's statistical relationship to outcomes while withholding its point-value weight — is not merely a confidentiality constraint observed for this paper; it is consistent with, and reinforces, how this project already discloses factor information externally, and the per-factor audit in Section 3.9 is itself a template for that kind of disclosure done with outcome evidence behind it.

## 4.6 Generalizability across regions and across generations

Two different generalization questions are addressed with two different strengths of evidence in this paper, and they should not be blurred together. Generalization across the panel's own regions is well supported: the KSI relative risk is stable in sign and rough magnitude across all 13 leave-one-region-out drops (Section 3.4), and the class-controlled severity effect strengthens rather than weakens under an independently re-scored model generation (Section 3.7). Generalization to the wider, 117-city index used by this project's routing product is supported by real but limited evidence, of a different kind than the road-level result above. Both the descriptive city rankings and the outcome-linked criterion validation reported in Section 3.11 come from the same, currently-published index generation as the rest of this paper. The marginal association the criterion validation finds (Spearman $\rho=-0.72$, KSI rate ratio 0.74 per +10 index points) is real and in the hypothesized direction, but it does not survive the same kind of control that leaves the road-level score's severity signal intact: country fixed effects collapse the city-level association to null (RR 1.00, $p=.983$), and three of the four countries with multiple indexed cities in the outcome data order inconsistently with the index. This is the reverse of the road-level result — the granular, segment-level score survives an analogous road-class control, while the coarser, city-aggregated index does not survive an analogous country control — and whether that reflects a genuine limitation of the index or non-comparable KSI definitions across the five countries in the outcome sample (Section 4.7) is not resolved here (Section 4.8). This paper's approach to that city-level question is not unique to this project. The question is whether an aggregated index actually predicts real outcomes, rather than assuming the aggregation is valid because the underlying road-level score is. A comparable composite road-safety index has been checked against crash prediction in Amman, where safety-weighted predictors gave the index a 69% correlation with observed road crashes [16]. A separate road safety index has been developed for the urban road network of Vijayawada [37], and a separate analysis of fatal and serious cycling collisions across the interconnected urban systems of the Liverpool City Region examined a related cross-boundary generalization question for cycling safety specifically [38]. The largest, best-known system of this kind for motor-vehicle-focused road infrastructure — the International Road Assessment Programme's star-rating methodology, deployed across many more countries than this paper's five-country panel covers — is a natural comparator this paper does not evaluate directly; its published outcome-validation evidence sits mainly in organisational and engineering-practice literature rather than in the peer-reviewed, DOI-indexed pool this paper's search drew from, and a direct comparison between the two systems' criterion validity is left as future work rather than attempted from a thin or ill-fitting citation.

## 4.7 Limitations

Several limitations bound how far these findings should be read. The counter panel likely under-samples the most dangerous end of the road network, because permanent automatic counters are disproportionately sited on roads cities want to promote rather than on roads this project's own score rates as genuinely dangerous, a pattern documented directly in this project's prior counter audits; hand counts on busier roads only partially correct this. The cluster-robust confidence intervals reported throughout should be read as approximate rather than exact: with only 13 regions of very unequal size, simulation evidence shows conventional cluster-robust inference performs markedly worse than under a balanced cluster structure of the same count [18], and a wild-cluster-bootstrap correction, the standard remedy in that literature [19], was not run for this paper. "Serious injury" is defined somewhat differently across the panel's five source countries, and while city fixed effects absorb much of that between-country difference, they do not absorb all of it. Germany contributes the largest share of KSI outcomes and of the per-factor audit's statistical power, so this paper's conclusions are more solidly grounded there than in the other four countries. The per-factor audit itself (Section 3.9) is univariate, testing each named factor's presence against KSI one factor at a time rather than jointly; the road-class control applied to the aggregate score in Section 3.7 is not applied factor-by-factor beyond the small set reported for Germany, so a factor's reported relative risk may still partly reflect its correlation with other, unreported factors rather than an independent effect. The relationship reported throughout is an association, not a demonstrated causal effect: this paper cannot show that routing a given cyclist along a lower-scored alternative would have changed that cyclist's outcome. The city-level index's failure to survive country-level control (Section 3.11) cannot be fully separated, with the evidence available here, from the cross-country non-comparability of "serious injury" definitions noted above: this paper's data cannot determine how much of that failure reflects a genuine limitation of the index's within-country discrimination versus an artifact of pooling five countries' differently-defined KSI outcomes into one regression. And, as with any source cited by DOI in this paper, a resolved DOI establishes that the cited work exists; it does not establish that the work supports the specific sentence citing it, a distinction this paper has tried to observe throughout but that a reader should hold onto independently.

## 4.8 Future research

Three directions follow directly from what this paper leaves open. Junction-specific risk modelling is the most consequential: the score's demonstrated severity signal is a mid-block signal, and closing that gap would need data this project's current segment-level design was not built to capture, such as signal-timing and sightline information that this project's own prior review of public map data concluded is not reliably available. Disentangling the two candidate explanations for the city-level index's failure under country control (Section 4.6) is the second: re-running the criterion validation within single countries only — Germany's Berlin against Stuttgart, or France's four indexed cities against one another, without pooling across borders — would test whether the index discriminates safety at all once the country-level confound is removed by design rather than by a fixed-effect adjustment, and a parallel harmonization of the "serious injury" definition across the outcome data's five source countries would test the competing explanation directly. And a more quantitative treatment of the underreporting mechanism proposed in Section 4.2 — using the reporting-rate gradient reported for cyclists specifically [13] to bound how much of this paper's frequency-null finding differential underreporting alone could produce — would turn a plausible co-mechanism into a testable, quantified claim rather than a stated possibility.

# 5. Conclusion

This paper set out to answer a question that is rarely asked in the form it deserves: does a deployed, safety-weighted cycling routing risk score predict real injury outcomes, and does it predict crash frequency and crash severity equally well, or differently? Using a five-country, 13-region counter-exposure panel of 2,034 sites, negative-binomial regression with cluster-robust standard errors and city fixed effects, and a replication under an independently re-scored later model generation, the answer is that it predicts them differently, and substantially so.

The score's relationship to raw crash frequency is weak, inconsistent across statistical estimators, and does not survive control for road classification in either generation examined. Its relationship to crash severity — killed-or-seriously-injured outcomes specifically — is the opposite on every axis tested: significant at every threshold and under every estimator, stable across all 13 leave-one-region-out checks, strengthening rather than weakening under a later, independently re-scored generation of the same methodology, and, for the first time across the generations compared here, robust to full road-class control on the complete panel. That surviving severity signal is concentrated at mid-block locations rather than at junctions, a finding this paper reports as a limitation of the score's current design rather than a caveat to be minimized.

Within what this paper's literature search found, this is the first validation of a deployed, multi-factor composite cycling risk score to test frequency and severity as two separable outcomes, at multi-country, multi-region scale, across more than one generation of the same scoring methodology. That contribution is methodological as much as empirical. The road-safety and cycling-safety literature this paper engaged with almost universally treats "crash risk" as one outcome. The pattern reported here suggests that collapsing frequency and severity into a single validated outcome can obscure a real, useful, and mechanistically well-grounded signal underneath an apparently weak pooled result. The evidence is most consistent with a Safe System kinetic-energy mechanism, plausibly compounded by severity-dependent underreporting of minor crashes in the official records this panel is built from — two explanations this paper's data cannot fully separate, and need not be treated as competing, since both would push the same finding in the same direction.

This paper has also tried to model a second kind of honesty alongside the statistical one: reporting a robustness check that does not favor the evidence being validated, rather than reporting only the checks that do. The city-level index's marginal association with real KSI outcomes is real, but it does not survive an analogous control to the one that leaves the road-level score's severity signal intact, and that result is reported in full (Section 3.11) rather than left out because it complicates a cleaner story. A validation study that runs a control only where it expects the control to pass is not more convincing for the omission; it is only harder to catch. Applying the same discipline to secondary evidence that the primary evidence was held to, as this paper has tried to do throughout Sections 2, 3, and 4, is a small consistency with an outsized effect on whether a reader can trust what is reported here.

The practical takeaway for a routing product built on a score of this kind is correspondingly specific rather than sweeping. A road scored worse by this system should be described, and used, as a road where a crash is more likely to be severe if one occurs, not as a road where a crash is more likely to occur at all. A published display scale should carry no more resolution than the outcome data can actually separate. Three directions remain open for future work: junction-specific risk modelling, which this paper's segment-level design does not address; disentangling whether the city-level index's failure under country-level control reflects a genuine limitation of the index or non-comparable injury definitions across the five outcome countries, which a single-country comparison and a harmonized severity definition could each test; and a more quantitative accounting of how much of the observed frequency-null result differential crash underreporting, rather than a true absence of effect, could explain on its own.
